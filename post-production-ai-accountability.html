    What the stack shows

    The lower layers follow rules a person wrote, so every step can be checked. The upper layers make probable guesses and are built from data nobody on the project saw. The higher the layer, the harder it is to say who decided what.

    The oldest layer can also help. Adobe stamps Generative Extend results with Content Credentials, and Netflix asks production partners to tell it before they use generative AI. Put those two ideas together and disclosure could be checked automatically before a show ships.

    Reel 7 · TC 02:00:00:00The courtrooms

    Who gets paid for the work the models learned from

    Authors, artists, studios, publishers and musicians are suing AI companies over what the models were trained on and what they produce. Courts have not agreed with each other. Only one big case has produced payments so far.

    Status as of late November 2026, from press reports. This is a summary for a report, not legal advice. Cases move quickly, so confirm each one on the court docket or with a lawyer before you quote it.

    $1.5BAnthropic settlement with authors, final approval July 2026
    70+suits filed by copyright owners against AI companies by the end of 2025
    3major studios suing Midjourney: Disney, Universal, Warner Bros. Discovery
    Nov 13Ontario Court of Appeal lets the news publishers' case against OpenAI go ahead

    The cases, by region

    CaseWho is suingWhat it is aboutWhere it stands
    United States
    Bartz v. AnthropicAuthorsBooks used to train an AI modelSettled
    A judge held that training on lawfully bought books was fair use and that downloading pirated copies was not. The case settled for $1.5 billion, with final approval in July 2026. About $3,000 per book, with first payments due the week of November 23, 2026 and more through September 2027.
    Kadrey v. MetaAuthorsBooks used to train Meta's modelsRuled
    Training was held fair use on that record because the authors showed no market harm. Claims about piracy continue. No appeal decision yet.
    Thomson Reuters v. Ross IntelligenceLegal publisherCopying Westlaw headnotes to train a legal research toolRuled
    The Third Circuit ruled on September 29, 2026 that this was not fair use. The court noted that Ross's AI was not generative. Ross says it will seek Supreme Court review.
    New York Times and other news publishers v. OpenAI and MicrosoftNewspapersTraining on articles, and what the chatbot outputsPending
    Summary judgment motions were argued in October 2026. I found no trial date. In September 2026 the US Justice Department filed a statement saying training should not expose developers to broad liability.
    Disney and Universal v. Midjourney, and Warner Bros. Discovery v. MidjourneyFilm studiosTraining on their films, and images of their charactersPending
    Midjourney says fair use. In July 2026 it asked for discovery into the studios' own use of AI. I found no ruling.
    Andersen v. Stability AI, Midjourney, Runway and DeviantArtVisual artistsImage models trained on artists' workPending
    A January 2026 filing lists a trial for April 5, 2027. Sources disagree on some dates.
    Lehrman v. LovoVoice actorsCloned voices sold as AI voiceoverRuled
    In July 2025 a judge let contract and right of publicity claims go ahead and dismissed most copyright and trademark claims. Commentators read it as pointing performers toward state law.
    Canada
    News publishers v. OpenAICanadian Press, Torstar, The Globe and Mail, Postmedia, CBC/Radio-CanadaNews content used to train ChatGPT without permission or paymentRuled
    Ontario's Superior Court kept the case in Ontario in November 2025. The Court of Appeal dismissed OpenAI's appeal on November 13, 2026. Coverage calls it the first Canadian case on training generative AI. The same publishers have also sued Anthropic.
    United Kingdom and Europe
    Getty Images v. Stability AIPhoto agencyImages used to train an image modelRuled
    The UK High Court ruled on November 4, 2025. Getty won a narrow trademark point. Commentary says the court found no copyright infringement because the model did not store the images.
    GEMA v. OpenAIGerman music rights societySong lyrics reproduced in outputsRuled
    A Munich court largely upheld GEMA's claims on November 11, 2025, including damages. Under appeal.
    GEMA v. SunoGerman music rights societyMusic used to train an AI music generatorRuled
    A Munich court largely upheld GEMA's claims on July 31, 2026. Commentary says it found infringement in both training and outputs.

    Status tags: Settled means money is changing hands. Ruled means a court has decided something, which may still be appealed. Pending means no decision on the main question.

    What the pattern says

    The fight is about two things: what the model was trained on, and what it produces. In the US, two judges so far have treated training on lawfully obtained books as fair use, while piracy and copying a rival product for a competing tool have gone the other way. Courts in Germany have sided more often with rights holders, and the UK ruling went the other way on whether a model stores images.

    Money has moved in one large case I found, the Anthropic settlement, and that was about pirated books. Disney and OpenAI announced a licensing deal for Sora in December 2025, and it ended in March 2026 when OpenAI shut Sora down. Reuters reported that no money had changed hands.

    The gap for people in post

    Nearly all of these cases are brought by authors, artists, publishers, studios and musicians. Editors, sound editors and VFX artists usually do not own the copyright in what they cut or build, since it commonly belongs to the production company. So the people closest to the tools may not be parties in any of these cases.

    Ask a Canadian entertainment lawyer how ownership works in your own contracts before you state this in the report.

    Reel 8 · TC 02:10:00:00The open question

    Where accountability goes missing

    A feature is one click inside a tool the artist already trusts. Follow the shot downstream and see who can know.

    Step 1

    Software vendor

    Knows what the model is and what trained it.
    Step 2

    Editor or artist

    Knows a button was pressed. May not know what sits behind it.
    Step 3

    Post house

    Sees the delivered shot. The method may not be logged.
    Step 4

    Studio or distributor

    Often relies on a signed deliverables sheet.
    Step 5

    Audience

    Told nothing on screen, in most cases.
    Scenario A

    The language swap

    One click dubs and subtitles a whole feature. Dialogue is replaced, the actor's voice may be cloned, the lips may be redrawn. Did the actor consent? Was the translation checked by a person?

    Scenario B

    The two second plate

    A stable establishing shot of New York in 1966, requested and delivered in minutes. No archive licence, no photographer, no credit. Is it presented as real? Who decided?

    Four questions every tool should be able to answer

    What changedDid it generate, alter or replace picture or sound?
    Whose workWhat data trained the model, and was it licensed?
    Whose face and voiceWas a performer's likeness copied or synthesized?
    Who approvedWho signed off, and where is that recorded?
